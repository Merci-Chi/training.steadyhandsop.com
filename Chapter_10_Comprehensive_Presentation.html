The Multi-Stakeholder Reality

In Local Business, "The Client" Is Rarely One Person

The Owner

  • Runs the business — your primary voice
  • Cares about: growth, time, pride, less hassle
  • Often the FIRST conversation, not the only one

The Spouse / Partner

  • Co-decides money in a huge share of local businesses
  • Cares about: household risk, "is this worth it?"
  • Usually joins LATE — or never, silently vetoing

The Gatekeeper / Manager

  • Answers the phone first — front desk, office manager
  • Filters access and shapes first impressions
  • An ally to win, never an obstacle to push past

Chapter 5's Authority question — "Who else needs to be involved?" — mapped this circle early. Chapter 10 is where you learn to work the whole circle as one system: a deal isn't won when one person is convinced. It's won when the circle is aligned.

Building Consensus

Navigating the Circle Without Politics

The Strategies

  • Common ground: frame the goal everyone shares — "more customers, less stress"
  • Progressive commitment: small yeses first — a preview, then a call, then the start
  • Arm your ally: give the owner the preview link and the one-liner to show the spouse
  • Invite, don't bypass: the deciding conversation happens WITH us present — never sold around anyone

The Dynamics to Respect

  • Never put the owner in an awkward spot before their partner — no "just sign now"
  • Never make the gatekeeper feel small — they shape the owner's mood before you're on
  • Seasonal cycles are political too: budgets, busy seasons, family calendars (Ch 5 Agenda)
  • Consensus built slowly holds; consensus forced quickly collapses

In family businesses, you're not just navigating a sale — you're navigating a relationship that outlasts your call. Handle the circle with respect and the whole circle remembers. Handle it carelessly and one "no" becomes three.

The Integration Principle

Frameworks Flow — They Don't Stack

The Novice's Stack

  • "First I do ALL of CGP… then ALL of TCI… then ALL of BAA…"
  • Checklist energy — the client feels processed
  • Rigid order breaks the moment reality interrupts
  • Frameworks as a script to recite

Steady Hands LLC's Flow

  • Frameworks interleave — guided by what the client says
  • A Budget answer mid-discovery? Noted and woven back — no whiplash
  • The client feels a great conversation — never a framework
  • Frameworks as instincts, not scripts (Ch 9's compounding promise)

The mastery indicator: in one deal you run DARE with the owner, tailor value for the spouse, respect the gatekeeper's timing — and run LACE on every objection that surfaces from any of them. Fluid, natural, and invisible. That's the summit.

Scenario 1 — The Salon Deal

Training Simulation — Multi-Stakeholder, Full Cycle

The Cast

  • Nina — owner, 5 years in business, phone constantly interrupting appointments
  • Marcus — her husband, handles the books, skeptical of "web people"
  • Dana — front-desk manager, answers every call first, protective of Nina's time

The Situation

  • Warm lead: Nina filled the interest form on steadyhandsop.com
  • Dana answers: "Can I ask what this is regarding?" — your first stakeholder moment is NOW
  • Nina available for 10 minutes only — between clients
  • Marcus will need to approve any monthly commitment
All names and numbers in these scenarios are fictional training simulations — in real calls, real people and their real numbers replace everything.

Scenario 1 — Move 1: Winning Dana

The Gatekeeper Is Your First Stakeholder

DANA: "Can I ask what this is regarding?" You: "Of course — Nina filled out a form on our website asking about getting the salon online. I'm following up on HER request. Is she seeing clients right now, or is there a better moment today?" — Transparent, respectful of Nina's time, gives Dana control. You're now Dana's ally, not her problem.
DANA: "She's with a client. She's super busy." You: "I figured — that's actually part of why she reached out. Could I grab ten minutes with her this afternoon, or would tomorrow morning be easier on the floor?" — You've shown Dana you understand the business's rhythm. She goes from gatekeeper to scheduler.
THE STAKEHOLDER LESSON Dana decides what Nina hears about you. A respectful 60 seconds with Dana buys a warm transfer. A pushy one buys "some salesperson is holding." Every person who answers the phone is a stakeholder.

Scenario 1 — Move 2: The 10-Minute Integrated Call

Frameworks Interleaving in Real Time

MINUTE 1–2: OPEN + CGP-C / SPIN-S "Nina — you asked about getting online. Before anything about us: walk me through how new clients find the salon today." → "Word of mouth, Instagram a little… but the new people moving in? They find the other salons."
MINUTE 3–4: CGP-G / DARE-D + TCI-T "What goal is that blocking — in numbers, roughly?" → "Maybe 4–5 new appointment requests a month? That's real money." → "And when do you want to feel that shift — before holiday season?" → "Yes. That's exactly the window."
MINUTE 5–6: TCI-C/I + DARE-R — THE HONEST MATH "And if it stays as it is?" → "The other salons just keep getting the new people." "And once they start finding YOU — what changes?" → "Fewer interruptions… and new faces. Growth." Then, HER numbers: ~4–5 missed clients × her average visit ≈ her monthly Gap. Conservative. Labeled. Hers.
MINUTE 7–8: BAA — Budget, Authority, Agenda "What would you plan to invest to fix this?" → "Honestly, Marcus watches the money — but if it made sense, sure." "Smart — it's a family decision. When we show you the preview, I'll give you what you need to show him in two minutes." Authority mapped. Ally armed.
MINUTE 9–10: VALUE CASE + NEXT-STEP CLOSE "Full site — built, customized, launched — $100 once. Hosting from $20/month. Against the gap you just described… you see the math. And you'll see a preview before anything goes live. Want me to get your preview started this week?" → "…Yes. Send it." Ask once. Stop talking.

Scenario 1 — Move 3: The Marcus Call

One Deal, Three Conversations — Each Tailored

The Setup

  • Nina shows Marcus the preview link that night — armed exactly as promised
  • Marcus calls with the skeptical question: "So what's this really going to cost me?"
  • Your stakeholder map says: lead with financial value + risk removal (Ch 6 tailoring)

The Conversation

  • "Fair question. $100 total to build it — one time. Then $20/month, everything included, cancel whenever. Nina can show you the exact preview — nothing hidden."
  • His math question? His wife's numbers — the Gap she stated. You never invented a figure
  • "And the preview already exists — you're not paying to find out what you'd get."
  • Marcus: "…That's less than I expected. Yeah. Go ahead."
The post-mortem: one deal, three conversations — Dana (respect + rhythm), Nina (discovery + her numbers + her close), Marcus (financial value + risk removal). The frameworks were invisible. The circle is aligned. That's integration.

Scenario 2 — The November Call

Training Simulation — Re-Engaging the Stalled Deal

The Backstory

  • Tom's Landscaping — warm discovery call back in June
  • Real interest, real Gap math done together — then: "Call me in November. Summer is our crazy season."
  • You logged it, honored it, and didn't pester once all summer (Ch 5 Agenda + Ch 7 discipline)
  • Now it's the first week of November

What Makes This Hard

  • Three months is a long time — has anything changed?
  • He may not remember the conversation's details
  • Re-pitching the old pitch ignores that HIS situation has moved
  • The kept promise is your opener — and your proof of character

Stalled deals are where average reps lose the thread and great reps harvest. The re-engagement is not a restart — it's a resumption of trust, and the trust was banked in June.

Scenario 2 — The Re-Engagement Play

Four Moves to Resume — Not Restart

1. HONOR THE PROMISE — the opener "Tom — it's [name] from Steady Hands. I promised I'd call in November, and here I am. How was the season?" — The kept promise IS the rapport. No re-introduction needed. Ask about HIS season first — you're a person who remembered, not a script that dialed.
2. RE-DISCOVER — re-run mini-CGP "Since June, what's changed on your end?" — Maybe he lost a crew. Maybe a competitor launched a site. Maybe winter bookings look thin. Re-run the discovery on today's reality — the June Gap is stale data until verified.
3. RE-WEIGH — fresh TCI, fresh numbers His new numbers, his new window: "You mentioned winter bookings are thin — so the timing you wanted is actually… now?" The Agenda he set in June becomes the urgency he owns in November. His calendar, his logic.
4. RE-CLOSE — the case, then the ask Rebuild the honest math on November's numbers. Then: "Based on all this — want the preview before the holidays?" Ask once. Stop. Or if it's a "not yet": "Fair — when's your spring planning? I'll call then, same promise." And mean it again.
Every kept-promise re-engagement compounds the Flywheel (Ch 8): even a second "not yet," handled this way, deepens the reputation that eventually refers.

Scenario 3 — The Burned Veteran

Training Simulation — Rebuilding Trust From Zero

The Backstory

  • Frank — auto detailing, 12 years in business
  • Paid another company $2,000 three years ago; site never worked right, support vanished
  • Cold call — and you're the third "web person" to call this month
  • His opening: "You people already took two grand off me once. Why would I talk to you?"

Why This Scenario Matters

  • It tests EVERYTHING: LACE order under hostility, the Banned List under temptation, trust-building with verifiable facts (Ch 7)
  • One wrong move — defensiveness, pushing, discounting — confirms his worldview
  • Done right, the burned veteran becomes the loudest advocate in the Flywheel — he has the most to gain from being wrong about you

Scenario 3 — The Trust Rebuild

LACE Under Fire — Verifiable Facts, Zero Pressure

L + A — ABSORB, THEN VALIDATE "…That would make me skeptical too. Two thousand dollars and a site that didn't work — I'd be done with this whole industry." — No defending the industry. No "but we're different." Feel his experience first. The pause after this line is doing more work than any pitch could.
C — CLARIFY THE REAL WOUND "Can I ask — what stung most? The money, or how you were treated after?" → "After. Vanished. Nobody picked up." — The real objection was never price. It was abandonment. Now you know exactly what to answer.
E — EXECUTE WITH VERIFIABLE FACTS ONLY "Here's what I can point to, and you can check every word: our prices are published — $100 to build, $20 a month, on the site right now. You see the preview before anything goes live. And our phone number is (702) 372-6399 — call it right now if you want. Someone answers." — Nothing to take on faith. Everything checkable.
THE SLOW CLOSE — AND THE HONEST OUT "I'm not going to push you tonight. Look at the pricing page, call the number, decide in your own time. If the answer's no — I respect it, and I won't call back to wear you down." → "…Send me the site." The slowest close in the book — and often the only honest one.
The temptation this scenario tests: inflating promises to "overcome" his skepticism. Never. With the burned veteran, verifiability is the only currency — and patience is the only technique.

The Role-Play Program

Where Steady Hands LLC team members Are Actually Made

How We Practice

  • Weekly sessions: one scenario per session — salon deal, November call, burned veteran, and ones your manager invents
  • Rotating roles: rep, client, gatekeeper, observer — playing the CLIENT teaches you what pitching feels like from the other side
  • Real constraints: 10-minute timer, interruptions, hostile openers — simulations include the chaos of real days
  • Escalating difficulty: master the friendly salon, then earn the burned veteran

The Rules of the Room

  • Stay in character until the debrief — no breaking to explain yourself mid-scene
  • Clients play it REAL: skeptical, busy, sometimes rude — kindness in role-play is cheating
  • Ethics count double in practice: run the Banned List scenarios so the temptation is familiar when it's real
  • Every rep practices before they perform — nobody's first LACE happens on a live prospect
The payoff: in the simulation you can fail safely. The awkward close, the skipped discovery, the defensive reflex — better to meet them here, with a teammate playing Frank, than on a live call that matters.

The Debrief Protocol

Where the Actual Learning Happens

1. SELF-REVIEW FIRST — the rep speaks first "What did I do well? What would I change?" — Self-assessment before external feedback builds the Ch 9 muscle. Never skipped.
2. OBSERVER FEEDBACK — specific, not general Not "it was good." Instead: "At minute four you jumped to the price before asking the Agenda question — that's the gap that shows up in the scorecard." Specific beats polite.
3. GROUP LEARNING — one insight harvested The room extracts one transferable lesson: "Gatekeepers respond to rhythm-questions — 'morning or afternoon?'" Posted to the team chat. Compounds for everyone.
4. ACTION PLAN — ONE change, next session "Next simulation: I will ask the Authority question before minute six." — One commitment, tracked next time. One per session — the same discipline as Ch 9's weekly review.
The loop mirrors everything: Learn → Apply → Adjust (Ch 9). Simulations ARE the Apply gear — and the debrief IS the Adjust. Skip the debrief and you've done theater, not training.
