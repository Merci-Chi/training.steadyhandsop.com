Objection Guide

Objection Handling Guide

The written reference for every objection you will hear — each one classified, decoded, and answered through LACE. Every card tells you what the objection usually really means, the full loop response, and what never to say. These are structures, not handcuffs — adapt the words, keep the order.

The Method — Read Once, Use Forever

Every objection on this site is answered with the same loop, in the same order. The order IS the ethics: understand before you answer.

L — Listen completelyA — Acknowledge the personC — Clarify to the REAL objectionE — Execute with value

The Five Rules of the Loop

The Loop Rules

  • One loop at a time — resolve, check ("does that address it?"), then handle the next. Three objections answered in one breath = none answered.
  • Clarify 1–3 times — the first objection stated is rarely the real one. Dig until you're confident.
  • Never argue — you can win a point and lose the deal in the same sentence.

The Conduct Rules

  • The price is the price — value answers price pressure, never discounts.
  • LACE order is non-negotiable — skipping to the answer is how brilliant responses hit the wrong target.

Value & Price Objections

At $100 once + $20–30/month, most "price" objections are really value objections — the math hasn't landed yet, or the wrong comparison is running in their head. Your job: find the comparison, fix it with their numbers.

"It's too expensive."

Usually really means: "I haven't yet seen value that justifies it" — or they're comparing against the wrong thing (free builders, or what they assumed websites cost).

L Let them finish completely. Their tone tells you which comparison they're running.
A "Fair to ask — most owners ask exactly that."
C "Sure — compared to what?" The reference point decides your entire answer. Wait for it.
E Branch on what they say:
Vs. free builders: "Then the real comparison is your time — and who fixes it at 9pm when it breaks. Free hands you the tools; we hand you the finished thing, built and supported."
Vs. expectations: "Most owners assume websites cost thousands. It's $100, one time — and hosting starts at $20 a month, everything included."
Vs. another quote: "What did they include? I'd rather you compare honestly — here's exactly what ours covers…"
Never say: "It's cheap!" — you just started an argument. · Any discount. · "You get what you pay for" — it insults everyone on the free route, including people they know.
"Why would I pay when Wix is free?"

Usually really means: "Would I have to build and manage it myself?" — it's a capacity question wearing a price costume (Ch 7, worked example).

L Let them finish: "…seems like everyone just uses those."
A "That's fair — free is worth a serious look. Honestly, for some people it's the right call."
C "When you picture your site — something you'd build and manage yourself, or something handled?" → "I don't have time to figure that out. I'd want it handled." That's the real objection.
E "Then that's exactly the difference. Free builders hand you the tools — you become the designer, the mobile-fixer, the support desk. We're the hands: built for you, mobile-ready, preview before launch, a real human at the end of the phone for $20 a month. If you'd enjoy building it yourself — genuinely, do the free route. If you want it handled — that's our $100." → "Does that make the difference clear?"
Never say: "Free sites look unprofessional" — someone they respect has one. · Trashing Wix by name — you're trashing their plan, not the tool.
"What's the catch?"

Usually really means: burned-before radar — they're waiting for the hidden fee to appear.

L+A "No catch — and I like that you checked."
C Usually unnecessary — the objection is direct. Answer it directly.
E "The $100 covers the full design and build. The preview is a starting point — we customize the final site for your business before it goes live. Hosting starts at $20/month — that part IS monthly, and I'd rather tell you now than surprise you later."
Never say: "There's nothing monthly!" — a lie that dies at invoice one and takes your reputation with it.
"Do it for $50 and I'll sign today."

Usually really means: a buying signal wearing a discount costume — they want in, and they're testing where your line is.

L+A "I appreciate a direct offer."
C Skipped deliberately — the objection is crystal clear. Spend the time on E.
E "The price is the price — $100 is what keeps real humans building and supporting it. What I can do is walk you through exactly what's included so you can judge the value yourself… And since you're ready to move — want me to get your preview started this week?" The "today" energy gets redirected to the proper close, not the discount.
Never say: "Let me ask my manager" theater. · Matching their number "just this once." · Guilt-tripping the offer.
"I don't have the budget right now."

Usually really means: EITHER a genuine constraint OR value-doubt in budget clothing. These need opposite answers — clarify which first.

L+A "Understood — and thanks for being straight about it."
C "Can I ask — is it the $100 upfront, the monthly, or honestly, not being sure yet that it's worth it?" All three answers lead somewhere different.
E
Genuine constraint: "Completely respected. When does your planning window open — next quarter, next season? I'll call then, and not before." Log it. Honor it exactly.
Value-doubt: back to THEIR Gap math (Ch 4–6): "Then let's check the math before we call it unaffordable — how many customers a month are walking past right now?"
Never say: pressure a genuine constraint — that's how rushed, resentful clients are made. · Inventing payment plans or "split it in two" — not official policy, ever.

Status Quo Objections

"I'm fine as I am" is the quietest deal-killer. The rule: honor what works, name the invisible gap. You never fight their engine — you position the site as the net underneath it. And if the status quo is genuinely comfortable? The honest answer is they may not need you yet — say so and keep the door open (TCI both-sides ethics).

"I already have a website."

Usually really means: "prove the change is worth the hassle."

L+A "Perfect — then you know what a site does for you."
C "Quick one: when you last pulled it up on your phone — how did it look? And when did someone last update it?"
E
Stale / broken on mobile: "That's the exact gap — the site exists but works against you. Many owners we meet are due a refresh; a preview costs nothing but a conversation."
Genuinely good site: honest correction + door open: "Then honestly, you may be in good shape — I'd rather tell you that than manufacture a problem. If anything changes — a redesign, hosting, support — we're at (702) 372-6399."
Never say: "That thing is terrible" — it's THEIRS. Criticize gently through questions, or not at all.
"Facebook / Instagram is enough for me."

Usually really means: it genuinely works for repeat business — the blind spot is new-customer search.

L+A "And it works — for people who already know you."
C The search test: "When someone NEW to the area types '[salon / cleaner / landscaper] near me' — where do you show up? … Right. Social profiles don't rank there. Websites do."
E "Think of it as rented land versus owned address: the feed changes its rules overnight — the site is yours, and it catches everyone the platforms miss. It's not instead of your page — it's the missing half."
Never say: "Facebook pages are unprofessional" — you're insulting their current strategy AND their skills.
"My nephew / my kid handles that."

Usually really means: free labor beats paying — until the first 9pm emergency, or the second year of neglect.

L+A "He might do a great job — genuinely."
C+E "The question is who's behind it at 9pm when it breaks — and who keeps it current on new phones. And if he's genuinely got it covered? Then that's fine — I'd rather you hear me say that than push. If you ever want it handled, we're one call away: (702) 372-6399."
Never say: anything against the nephew — that's family, and you just made an enemy. · Implying they're cheap for using him.
"I'm booked out anyway."

Usually really means: no felt pain right now — TCI honest answers confirm it. That's a qualification RESULT, not a failure.

L+A "Love that — genuinely."
C "Is that every month, or does it dip?" — seasonality usually opens the door.
E "If you're truly full year-round, a website isn't urgent — I'd rather tell you that than push. Most owners do say the phone-answering alone — hours, pricing, what's included — earns its keep. And when the dip comes, you'll know where I am."
Never say: "You're NOT that busy" — arguing a busy owner out of their own reality loses every single time.
"All my work is word of mouth / repeat clients."

Usually really means: the referral engine works — and they've never examined what happens AFTER a referral is given.

L+A "Word of mouth is the best marketing there is — no argument."
C+E "Here's the quiet part: every referral looks you up before they call. What do they find? … Exactly. The site doesn't replace word of mouth — it closes the people the word of mouth sends. And it catches the new arrivals who don't know anyone here yet."
Never say: "Word of mouth isn't enough" — it clearly IS for them; that's why they said it. You'd be arguing with their lived success.

Timing & Agenda Objections

The rule of this whole tab: timing pushback gets scheduled, not steamrolled. Agenda objections are scheduling information, not rejection (Ch 5). Forcing timing creates rushed, unhappy clients — and rushed clients churn, which costs more than the sale earned.

"Now's not a good time."

Usually really means: either a real busy window — or a soft no. One question sorts them.

L+A "Totally understandable."
C "When IS a better window?" — the answer separates real windows from polite brushes.
E
Real window: "Then [month] it is — I'll call then, and not before." Log the exact date. Honor it exactly.
Vague brush: one soft probe: "In general — are mornings or afternoons easier to reach you?" Then honor whatever they give. No third attempt this cycle.
Never say: "It only takes a minute!" — their time is not yours to budget. · Calling before the promised window "just to check in."
"Let me think about it."

Usually really means: one of three unresolved questions. "Thinking" is where deals go to die — unless you find the real question.

L+A "Of course."
C "So I send you off with the right things to think about — which part deserves the thinking: the $100, the monthly, or whether it'll actually help?"
E Answer the one they name with the matching tool:
The $100: what's included, the preview process — value for the build.
The monthly: the flexibility policy — plans move as needs change; hosting is what keeps it alive.
Whether it'll help: their own Gap math, one more time — their numbers, not yours.
Never say: "What's there to think about?" · Filling their silence with a re-pitch.
"Call me in November." (any season/month)

Usually really means: a genuine scheduling instruction — and the most valuable data point in your CRM.

L+A+E "November it is. Anything changes before then, you have my number."
THE SYSTEM Log the date. Return ON the date — not before, not "a week early to be safe." The kept promise becomes the warmest opener in sales: "I promised I'd call in November, and here I am." (Ch 10, Scenario 2 — the Tom play: honor → re-discover → re-weigh → re-close.)
Never do: the October "just checking in" call. You promised November. The promise IS the pipeline.

Authority Objections

In local business, decisions are family decisions more often than not. The rule: bring the circle in — never sell around it. Chapter 5 asked "Who else is involved?" early; these cards handle when the answer surfaces late.

"I need to ask my husband / wife."

Usually really means: a real co-decision structure — not an excuse in the vast majority of local businesses.

L+A "Smart — it's a family decision." (Never condescend. Never sigh.)
C Usually unnecessary — the structure is clear. Move straight to arming the ally.
E Two paths, offer both:
Arm the ally: "What's helped other owners: I send you the preview link — you show them in two minutes."
Invite the circle: "Or if it's easier, let's get them on a quick call — I'd rather answer their questions myself than have you guess them."
When the spouse joins: switch the lead value to financial + risk removal — their numbers, the preview, the flexibility policy (Ch 6 tailoring).
Never say: "Why do you need permission?" · Pressuring them to decide alone tonight. · Calling before the promised follow-up time.
"It's not my decision — I just answer the phones."

Usually really means: a gatekeeper stating their role. They are an ally to win — never an obstacle to push past.

L+A "Got it — thanks for being straight with me."
C "Is there generally a good time [Owner] takes calls about the business?"
E "Could I leave the essentials with you — my name, the website, and the number, (702) 372-6399? If [Owner] asks about the website request, that's everything they'd need. And thank you for guarding their time — that's exactly the right instinct."
Never say: "It's a personal call" — tricking a gatekeeper burns the bridge permanently. · Demanding the owner's cell. · Treating the handoff as beneath you.

Trust Objections

The pattern for this entire tab: insistence builds doubt; verifiability builds trust. Never argue your credibility — point to things the prospect can check themselves: the published prices, the preview process, the real phone number. Full training: Ch 7, Slide 16.

"Never heard of you guys."

Usually really means: unknown = risky until proven otherwise. Fair position.

L+A "Fair — no reason you would have yet."
C Usually unnecessary.
E "We're a Las Vegas web company, and businesses like yours are exactly who we serve. Everything's at steadyhandsop.com — prices published right on the site. And our number is (702) 372-6399 — call or text anytime."
Never say: "We're the best in Vegas!" — unverified superlatives ARE the thing they're guarding against.
"How do I know you won't take the money and vanish?"

Usually really means: the #1 wound of this market — abandonment by a previous provider.

L+A "That's the right question to ask anyone in this industry."
C Usually unnecessary.
E "Because you see the actual site before launch — the preview is built first, before anything goes live. The monthly only continues if you're happy. And the phone number is real: (702) 372-6399 — call it right now if you like. Someone answers."
Never say: "How dare you suggest—" — the suspicion was earned by the industry, not by you. Absorb it without taking it personally.
"Sounds too good to be true."

Usually really means: healthy skepticism doing its job.

L+A+E "Then don't take my word for it — check the pricing page, call the number, decide in your own time. No pressure from me either way."
THE MECHANISM The confident withdrawal IS the credibility. Salespeople who push past this objection confirm it was too good to be true. The one who says "go verify" gets verified — and verification favors us every time, because everything about us checks out.
Never say: "I promise!" · Any new, bigger claim to overcome the doubt.
"You people already took two grand off me once." — the burned veteran

Usually really means: industry trauma. The deepest objection in our market — and handled right, the source of our loudest future advocate (Ch 10, Scenario 3 — Frank).

L+A Full absorb, no defending: "…That would make me skeptical too. Two thousand dollars and a site that didn't work — I'd be done with this whole industry." Then pause. The pause does more work than any sentence could.
C "Can I ask — what stung most? The money, or how you were treated after?" → almost always: "After. Vanished. Nobody picked up." The real wound is abandonment, not price.
E Verifiable facts ONLY: "Here's what I can point to, and you can check every word: our prices are published — $100 to build, $20 a month, on the site right now. You see the preview before anything goes live. And our number is (702) 372-6399 — call it right now. Someone answers."
THE SLOW CLOSE "I'm not going to push you tonight. Look at the pricing page, call the number, decide in your own time. If the answer's no — I respect it, and I won't call back to wear you down." → "…Send me the site."
Never say: anything defending the industry. · Matching his energy. · Rushing the close. With the burned veteran, patience is the only technique and verifiability is the only currency.

Special Cases & The Hard Lines

"Is this a scam?" / "Are you legit?"

Usually really means: caller-ID fatigue — everyone with a phone is braced for fraud daily.

L+A+E "It's a real sales call from a real Las Vegas company — I'd rather say that straight. Check us before deciding: steadyhandsop.com, prices published, (702) 372-6399. If anything smells off to you, trust that instinct — and we part friends."
Never say: over-protesting ("Scam?! How could you even—") · getting defensive. Calm transparency is the whole answer.
"Stop calling me." — the clear hard no

Usually really means: exactly what it says. This is a legal and ethical instruction — the end of the line, absolutely.

THE ENTIRE RESPONSE "Understood — you won't hear from me again. Have a good one." Then: log do-not-contact in the CRM immediately.
The absolute line: no "one more call in six months." No switching reps to get around it. No new number. The Banned List's "wearing them down" ends HERE, without exception. One clean exit protects the company's legal standing and your own integrity — and the door stays theirs to open, not yours to knock on.
"Can you also do SEO / ads / social media?"

Usually really means: a real need — outside your lane.

E "That's outside what I handle personally — let me connect you with our team." Official channels: (702) 372-6399 · kiara@steadyhandsop.com. Escalate and log it.
Never say: "Sure, we can figure that out." One improvised promise can undo a hundred honest calls (Ch 3).
When YOU make a mistake on the call

What it means: wrong name, wrong detail, misspoke about a price. It happens. The handling is the reputation.

THE MOVE Own it instantly: "That's my mistake — [correct name], thank you for catching it." Correct the record clearly. Continue the conversation normally.
A mistake owned in one sentence builds more trust than ten minutes of smooth perfection. A mistake hidden or glossed erodes everything. Never blame "the CRM" or "the system" — it was your call.
The talker who won't let you ask anything

Usually really means: they're ENGAGED — this is a good problem requiring gentle structure.

E — redirect "I love that you're telling me all this — let me ask one structured question so I can actually use it: how do new customers find you today?"
E — or schedule "There's a lot here and I want to do it justice — can we book fifteen minutes tomorrow?"
Never do: talk over them. Their monologue contains your discovery — extract the CGP material from it and reflect it back.

The Tab in One Breath

Always

  • LACE in order — even under hostility
  • Verifiable facts over insistence
  • Own your mistakes in one sentence
  • Honor every "stop calling" absolutely

Never

  • Defend, argue, or match hostile energy
  • Call around a do-not-contact instruction
  • Improvise capabilities beyond the official offer
  • Hide a mistake to protect the moment
