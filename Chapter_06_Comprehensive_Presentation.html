Crafting Compelling Value Propositions

Turning a $100 Website Into an Undeniable Investment

A value proposition is the bridge between what we offer and what the client needs to achieve. Done right, $100 + $20/month stops being a cost — and becomes the obvious answer.

The FormulaVALUE = BENEFIT − COST
QuantifiedHonest numbers from the client's own world
TailoredThe right story for each decision-maker

What a Value Proposition Is

A Clear Statement of Tangible Results

What It Is Not

  • A list of features: "10 pages, mobile-ready"
  • A slogan: "We build great websites"
  • A price announcement: "It's only $100"
  • A promise of guaranteed results

What It Is

  • A clear statement of the tangible results the client gets
  • Built on their stated challenge and goal (CGP)
  • Quantified with their own numbers
  • An investment case they can verify themselves

The test: could the client repeat your value proposition to their spouse in one sentence — and have it make sense? If not, it's not a value proposition. It's a pitch.

What Makes One Compelling

Five Requirements — All Five, Every Time

1. Specific

  • Concrete language, no vague benefits
  • "More calls" → "New clients finding you on Google" ✓

2. Relevant

  • Directly addresses THEIR stated challenge
  • The salon story ≠ the landscaper story

3. Quantified

  • Their numbers: clients missed, average visit value
  • Numbers they gave you — not invented ones

4. Unique

  • Differentiates from competitors, DIY, and doing nothing
  • The preview → customize → launch process is ours

5. Believable

  • Conditional math, not inflated promises
  • "If even half of what you described happens…" — credibility is currency

The Value Formula

Simple Math — Honest Math

VALUE = BENEFIT − COST
Value must be clearly positive — and visibly better than every alternative

BENEFIT

  • New clients captured
  • Interruptions eliminated
  • Professional presence, pride

COST — All of It

  • $100 one-time
  • $20–30/month hosting
  • Their time: one conversation + preview review

VALUE — The Gap

  • The distance Benefit − Cost
  • Expressed in THEIR dollars and hours
  • Compared against alternatives honestly

The formula only works when the Cost side is fully honest — including the monthly commitment. Hiding recurring costs to shrink "COST" kills trust the moment the first invoice arrives.

The Four Types of Value

Stack All Four — Price Conversations Shrink

Financial Value

  • New clients who find and book them online
  • Revenue recovered from the Gap they described
  • Cost of inaction vs. $100 + $20–30/mo

Strategic Value

  • Visible where competitors are visible — or more so
  • "Established business" perception for a $100 build
  • An owned address vs. rented Facebook land

Operational Value

  • Hours & prices answered by the site, not the phone
  • Fewer interruptions during appointments
  • Backend plan: update prices/services themselves in minutes

Emotional Value

  • Pride in sharing their web address
  • Peace of mind — someone answers when something breaks
  • Confidence that growth isn't limited to word of mouth
A client who only sees Financial value haggles over $20/month. A client who feels all four sees the complete picture — and the monthly cost shrinks inside it.

Building One: The 6-Step Process

From Discovery Data to a One-Sentence Case

1. DISCOVER (CGP + DARE) Challenges, goals, plans — and the quantified Gap. Without this, nothing that follows is tailored.
2. IDENTIFY VALUE TYPES Map their situation to all four: financial, strategic, operational, emotional. Which matter most to THEM?
3. DIFFERENTIATE What makes our answer unique vs. competitors, DIY, and doing nothing? (Preview process, real humans, honest pricing.)
4. QUANTIFY Attach THEIR numbers to every benefit possible — conservatively, conditionally.
5. EVIDENCE Industry-matched real clients, the preview process, verified facts only. Never invented results.
6. CRAFT THE MESSAGE One sentence they could repeat to their spouse. Then the supporting structure around it.

Quantification — The Honesty Rules

Numbers Build the Case. Invented Numbers Destroy It.

The Rules

  • The client supplies the inputs — clients missed, average visit value, hours lost
  • You facilitate the math; they watch it happen
  • Round conservatively — understate, never inflate
  • Frame conditionally: "IF the site captures even two of those four…"
  • Label everything as their estimate: "rough," "ballpark," "your numbers"

Why This Wins

  • They can't argue with their own numbers
  • Conditional framing = no broken promises later
  • Conservative math that still works = unanswerable case
  • When results arrive, you UNDER-promised — trust compounds
The difference between quantification and manipulation: quantification uses the client's own conservative estimates with an IF. Manipulation uses your inflated numbers with a WHEN. We do the first — always.

The Honest Math — Worked Example

Maria's Salon Numbers, From Chapter 4

Her estimate: new clients lost to competitors~4 / month
Her estimate: average new-client visit~$45
The Gap (her math, her words)~$180 / month walking past
Investment: development$100 one-time

Notice the structure: her numbers, conservative scenario, explicit IF, break-even honestly labeled. This case is unanswerable precisely because it refuses to exaggerate.

Before & After Framing

Paint the Transformation — With Their Colors

BEFORE — their current state, in their words "New residents search 'salon near me,' find three competitors, and never know you exist. Meanwhile your phone interrupts every appointment with hours-and-prices calls."
TRANSFORMATION — what changes, mapped to each pain "A complete site puts you in that search — services, prices, photos, hours — answering those questions for you, 24/7. Built, customized and launched for you."
AFTER — the future state, conditionally quantified "IF even 2 of those 4 lost clients a month find you instead — that's roughly $90/month recovered, against $20/month hosting. And your phone gets quieter."
The Before must use THEIR words from discovery — never your assumptions. If you described their "before" wrong, they'll discount everything that follows.

Tailoring to Each Decision-Maker

The Local Business Decision Circle

The Owner

  • Cares about: growth, pride, time, fewer headaches
  • Lead with: operational + emotional value
  • "Your site answers the repetitive calls so you can work"

The Spouse / Partner

  • Cares about: household money, risk, "is this worth it?"
  • Lead with: financial value + low risk
  • "$100 once, $20/month — against the $180/month Maria-math. And there's a preview before anything goes live."

The Gatekeeper / Manager

  • Cares about: making their boss happy, their own workload
  • Lead with: how it makes THEM look and life easier
  • "When the boss asks 'why don't we show up on Google' — you'll have the answer ready."

Same offer. Same numbers. Different lead value for each listener. Chapter 5's Authority question ("Who else is involved?") tells you who's in the circle — this slide tells you what to say to each.

Anatomy of a Value Proposition

Four Building Blocks — In Order

1. THE HEADLINE — one sentence, their outcome "Your salon, findable and bookable 24/7 — built and launched for you." If they remember nothing else, they remember this.
2. THE CONTEXT — their situation, mirrored back "You told me new clients search online and find the other salons — and your phone never stops during appointments." Proof you listened.
3. THE SUPPORTING POINTS — 2–3, each tied to a pain "Full design and build, mobile-ready — $100 one-time. Hosting from $20/month keeps it fast and online. Preview before launch — you see it before anyone else does."
4. THE PROOF — honest and verifiable "We do this for local businesses — cleaning services, salons like Studio 47, landscapers." Real client industries, verified facts, zero invented numbers.

Competitive Differentiation

Three Alternatives — Answer All Three

vs. Other Web Companies

  • Their move: big quotes, long timelines, upsell ladders
  • Our case: $100 done-for-you, days not months, real humans on the phone
  • "No $2,000 proposals. No 6-week waits."

vs. DIY / "My Nephew"

  • Their move: free or cheap, but who maintains it?
  • Our case: built + hosted + maintained + supported — for the price of a dinner out
  • "Who fixes it at 9pm when it breaks? We do."

vs. Doing Nothing

  • Their move: "it's worked so far"
  • Our case: the Gap keeps compounding — every month is another ~4 clients to the competitors
  • Presented as their own math — never as fear
Differentiation rule: elevate YOUR case — never trash the alternative. "Your nephew might do a great job" keeps respect; "your nephew will ruin it" burns the bridge and your credibility.

Logic Leads, Emotion Amplifies

The Balancing Act

The Sequence

  • 1. Lead with logic: the quantified case — their numbers, the math
  • 2. Amplify with emotion: connect the math to their life
  • 3. Support with evidence: preview process, real client industries

Emotional Value — Said Honestly

  • "Imagine handing out a web address you're proud of."
  • "Sleep easy knowing someone answers when the site needs help."
  • "Your business, presented the way you'd present it in person."

Owners buy on emotion and justify with math — so give them both, in that order. But the emotion must be real: pride, relief, confidence. Manufactured urgency or fear is banned in this program.

Proof Points — Used Ethically

What You Can Say, and What You Never Say

Safe & True

  • "We build and host sites for local businesses — a cleaning company, a hair salon, a landscaping company."
  • "The process: you see a preview, we customize, then launch."
  • "Hosting starts at $20/month — support when you need it."
  • "Plans are flexible — you can move between hosting levels as your needs change."

Never

  • Invented results: "Studio 47 got 50 new clients!"
  • Fabricated quotes or fake testimonials
  • "Guaranteed first page on Google"
  • Any statistic you can't trace to a real source

If they ask for proof you don't personally hold: "Great question — I can connect you with our team for references." Honesty about proof limits is itself proof of character.

Presenting It On the Call

A 90-Second Structure

SECOND 0–20: MIRROR THEIR SITUATION "So — no site, new residents finding the competitors, and the phone interrupting every appointment. Did I get that right?" Their nod is your permission to proceed.
SECOND 20–50: THE CASE Headline → the honest math (their Gap vs. $100 + $20/mo) → the four value types that matter most to THEM.
SECOND 50–75: THE PROOF & THE PATH Real client industries + the preview process. "You'd see a preview before anything goes live — you approve it first."
SECOND 75–90: THE CHECK "Based on what you've told me — does that math work for your situation?" Then STOP. Silence invites their real reaction.

Testing & Refining

Value Propositions Are Living Things

How to Test Yours

  • Client reactions: silence? confusion? "that makes sense"?
  • Which benefits make them lean IN — track it per industry
  • A/B across calls: lead a salon with financial value once, emotional next — compare responses
  • Ask directly: "Which part of that matters most to you?"

When to Update

  • An industry pattern emerges (salons lean emotional, landscapers lean visual-strategic)
  • Objections repeat — the value prop missed something
  • Team debriefs: share what's landing and what's not
  • Quarterly: re-verify every claim is still accurate to our offers

When They Push Back on the Value

Three Common Challenges — First-Pass Responses

"That math sounds too good to be true." "Fair — and that's why every number in it is yours, not mine, and why I said IF. Here's the worst case: you get a complete professional site for $100 and $20/month. Here's your scenario: the math you just watched me build. Judge it yourself."
"I need to see proof first." "Absolutely reasonable. The strongest proof we can give you today is the preview process — you see YOUR site before it goes live, and you don't pay for promises. I can also connect you with our team for references."
"My business is different." "You're right — and that's exactly why I didn't use a generic pitch. Everything I showed you came from YOUR numbers. Let's adjust the math together if any of my assumptions are off."
Full objection-handling mastery — LACE, the Value Shield, negotiation — is Chapter 7. These are first-pass responses; the deep framework comes next.

Value Prop Mistakes to Avoid

Six Ways Good Reps Weaken Good Cases

Construction Mistakes

  • Generic: identical pitch to every industry
  • Unquantified: "it will help a lot" — help whom, how much?
  • Feature-led: opening with specs instead of their outcome

Integrity Mistakes

  • Inflated math: big numbers you can't stand behind
  • Hidden costs: burying the monthly hosting — trust dies at invoice one
  • Borrowed proof: invented results for named real clients

The self-audit, every call: "Could this client repeat my value proposition to their spouse — accurately — right now?" If yes, you built it right. If no, simplify until they can.

Chapter 6 — Summary

What You Must Walk Away With

The Craft

  • VALUE = BENEFIT − COST — with all costs stated honestly
  • Stack all four value types: financial, strategic, operational, emotional
  • Quantify with THEIR numbers — conservative, conditional, labeled
  • Structure: headline → context → supporting points → proof

The Integrity

  • Conditional framing (IF) — never guaranteed promises
  • Real client industries — never invented results
  • Monthly cost always visible — never buried
  • Tailor the lead value to each decision-maker in the circle

Quick Check

Test Yourself Before Moving On

1. In VALUE = BENEFIT − COST, what must the "COST" side include?

Only the $100 development fee Everything: the $100 one-time, the $20–30/month hosting, AND the client's time investment Whatever makes the value look biggest

Full honesty on cost — including recurring — is what keeps the value proposition credible at invoice time.

2. Building the quantified case, the correct approach is:

Use industry averages, stated confidently, to maximize the gap The client supplies the numbers; you facilitate conservative math, frame it with IF, and label it as their estimate Quote a fixed guaranteed return to build confidence

Their numbers + conservative rounding + conditional framing = a case they can't argue with and you never have to walk back.

3. The owner loves the pitch but says "my wife handles the money." You should:

Pressure him to decide alone tonight Skip the spouse — hope for the best at close Get the spouse on the next call, and prepare the FINANCIAL + low-risk version of the value case for them (preview process, flexible plans, their own numbers)

Chapter 5's Authority question found the circle; Chapter 6's tailoring gives each member the version of value they need to hear.
